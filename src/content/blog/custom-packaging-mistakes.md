Most problems in custom packaging orders are created before the first carton is made. They are specification problems, approval problems and ownership problems — and all three are avoidable in the brief. Here are the seven that come up most.

## 1. Ordering samples before the specification is finished

A sample priced against an unfinished brief is a sample of the wrong product. Every structural or material change after the first sample restarts the sampling stage. Write the dimensions, material, print finish and quantity range first, and sample once.

## 2. Quoting on different structures and comparing unit prices

A corrugated mailer, a rigid box and a folding carton are different products with different setup economics. Compare quotations for the same structure, material and finish — or convert everything to a landed cost before comparing anything.

## 3. Ignoring the carton dimensions until freight is quoted

Freight is paid on space and weight. A retail box that ships flat can halve the freight carried by each unit; a box that ships assembled does not. Carton size affects the landed cost more than a small change in the factory price — ask for the carton data before the order, not after.

## 4. Not stating who owns the artwork and the tooling

Print plates, cutting dies and structural designs are quotable assets. If the quotation is silent, ownership stays where it started: with the factory. State in the quotation that artwork, plates and tooling are owned by you or priced as transferable, so a second order does not restart from a captive supplier.

## 5. Approving samples by email summary

"Looks good" is not an approval. Sample approval should go through the written checkpoints: dimensions, material, colour, print registration, finish, and the packed result. If the sample that ships differs from the one approved, the written checkpoint is what you have.

## 6. Forgetting the compliance questions in the destination market

Materials, inks and labelling for a U.S. retail product can carry requirements — food-contact statements, warnings, recycled-content claims — that belong in the specification, not discovered after production. Compliance reading belongs with the appointed specialist; the brief should flag what needs checking before tooling is made.

## 7. Treating the first order as the whole relationship

The first order prices the setup: plates, dies, sampling, learning. The second order prices the production. If you plan repeat orders, say so in the brief — the quotation can spread setup economics differently when a run continues, and the unit price on a repeat order is the honest comparison point.

## The one-line version

Write the complete brief, quote like against like, get the carton data, own your tooling, approve against checkpoints, check compliance early, and plan for the second order before the first one ships.
