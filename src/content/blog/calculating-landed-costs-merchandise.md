Landed cost combines the goods price with the order-specific costs of bringing the goods to an agreed destination. For a packaging or textile purchase, list product cost, setup, transport, insurance where applicable, import charges and final delivery. Mark missing amounts as unknown, avoid counting included costs twice, and allocate the total over a clearly stated quantity.

The exact items, rates, and party responsible depend on the product, origin, Incoterm, shipment method, importer of record, and destination. Treat this as a planning checklist, not a customs or tax determination.

## What is Landed Cost?
The landed cost is the combined cost of the goods and the services required to bring them to the delivery point agreed in the order. It should be calculated against the same quantity and delivery terms as the supplier quote.

### The Landed Cost Formula
**Landed Cost = Product cost + International transport + Insurance (if applicable) + Import charges + U.S. handling/delivery + Order-specific costs**

Before approving a production run, ask for each item to be shown separately whenever possible.

## 1. Product and production costs
Confirm the unit price, quantity, tooling or setup, sample charges, artwork changes, packaging of the finished goods, payment terms, and the Incoterm. A lower unit price can be less useful if it excludes an essential cost or only applies to a different specification.

## 2. Transport and insurance
Request a transport quote that matches the final carton count, dimensions, weight, pickup point, delivery point, and preferred shipping method. For both packaging and textiles, the way goods are packed can materially affect the chargeable volume. Insurance and delivery services should be stated separately if they are not included.

## 3. Classification and import charges
Product classification, duty treatment, tariffs, and import responsibilities must be checked against the exact product and current rules. The [U.S. International Trade Commission's HTS](https://hts.usitc.gov/) is the official tariff schedule; when binding guidance is required, U.S. Customs and Border Protection explains how to request a classification ruling through its [eRulings process](https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1107?language=en_US).

Do not rely on a generic rate or an AI estimate for an import decision. Confirm the classification, applicable charges, and importer obligations for the specific order. A customs broker may be used, but CBP notes that the importer remains responsible for the entry information and duties even when a broker is involved.

See [CBP's explanation of importer responsibility](https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1169?language=en_US). Confirm the current treatment of the specific product and shipment with the responsible professional; the example below deliberately supplies no tariff percentage.

## 4. U.S. handling and order-specific costs
Depending on the agreed route and terms, the budget may also include port or terminal handling, domestic delivery, storage, inspections arranged by the buyer, or other order-specific services. These should be confirmed in the commercial terms rather than assumed.

## A worked example with unknown import charges

The following figures are fictional USD amounts for explaining the arithmetic. They are not a quote, market-average costs, a previous order or a tariff estimate. Assume 2,000 units and that no listed amount is already included in another line.

| Cost input | Hypothetical amount | Basis to confirm on a real order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goods: 2,000 × $2.40 | $4,800 | Same specification and quantity |
| Setup allocated to this order | $300 | Whether already included in product price |
| Origin handling and international transport | $1,100 | Final packing and route |
| Insurance | $60 | Coverage and inclusion |
| Destination handling and delivery | $500 | Named delivery point and services |
| Import charges | Unknown: D | Product-specific professional review |
| Total before unresolved import charges | $6,760 | Not a complete landed cost |

The planning expression is **($6,760 + D) ÷ 2,000 = $3.38 + D ÷ 2,000 per unit**. Additional costs identified during the review must also be added. The unresolved amount is visible instead of being silently treated as zero.

As a sensitivity exercise only, adding a fictional $800 cost allowance increases the planning amount by $0.40 per unit. That allowance is not a calculated duty or evidence that $800 covers the import charges. Replace it with confirmed inputs before relying on the budget.

## How do you avoid double counting and misleading unit costs?

Use the delivery basis of the supplier quote to identify which transport and handling charges are already included. The [ICC's Incoterms guidance](https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/incoterms-2020/) explains the allocation of cost, risk and obligations. Record the chosen rule, named place and edition with the quote. A trade-term abbreviation alone should not replace a complete discussion of the order.

Keep order quantity and expected usable quantity separate. In the fictional example, a planning scenario allocating $6,760 over 1,900 usable units gives about $3.56 per unit before D. This is a sensitivity scenario, not an expected defect rate. It demonstrates why the denominator must be stated.

For several products, record the allocation method for shared freight or setup. An allocation by quantity may differ from one by volume or weight. Select a method appropriate to the decision and use it consistently across proposals.

## What makes a cost sheet ready for a decision?

Every required amount should have a value or a clearly recorded unresolved item, an inclusion check, a source date and a responsible person. Check quotation validity and confirm changes to materials, packing or route before accepting the total.
