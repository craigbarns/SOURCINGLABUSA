Sampling and tooling are where custom orders get expensive quietly. Neither is expensive in absolute terms; both are expensive when they are repeated, misunderstood, or owned by someone else. Here is what each one is and what to confirm before approving.

## What a sample actually costs

A sample price usually bundles three things: making the physical sample, shipping it, and the setup work behind it — artwork preparation, colour matching, sometimes a cutting die. In packaging, a printed sample can involve plates that are also production tooling; in apparel, a prototype sample involves pattern work that becomes the production pattern.

Ask for the sample quotation to state:

- What the sample costs, and whether it is credited against the production order
- What setup steps it includes, and which of those are reusable
- What the production price becomes after sample approval

## What tooling is, and why it belongs in the quotation

Tooling is the set of dedicated assets your product needs before mass production: cutting dies, print plates, embossing or hot-foil tooling, moulds, marking fixtures, production patterns and tech packs. It is usually quoted once and amortized over a run.

Two quotations for the same product can differ exactly by their tooling treatment: one includes it in the unit price, one lists it separately. Neither is wrong — but only the separate version lets you see the setup economics and plan a second order against them.

## The three ownership questions

1. **Who owns the tooling after payment?** If the quotation is silent, the factory does. State that tooling is owned by you upon payment, or priced as transferable.
2. **Can it be moved?** Tooling that lives at one factory's site can be a soft lock-in. Ask where it resides and what it costs to transfer it.
3. **Who holds the artwork and the master pattern?** Print files, dielines and graded patterns are as much your asset as the steel. Ask for copies at approval, not at the end of the relationship.

## Sample cycles: where timelines and budgets die

Each additional sample cycle adds making and shipping time, which often exceeds the production time of the approved sample. The fix is procedural, not luck: send complete written comments in one pass — dimensions, material, colour, print registration, finish, packed result — and define what "approved" means before the sample ships.

## What to put in the brief

- Sample cost, and whether it is credited against production
- Tooling list, price, ownership upon payment, and where it resides
- What happens to the artwork and master pattern after approval
- The production price after sample sign-off

A quotation that answers these four things is quotable. One that does not is a price on a website.
