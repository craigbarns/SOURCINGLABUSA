Tariffs on Chinese-origin goods change by government action, sometimes several times a year. Any article that prints "the current tariff" without a date checked is out of date within weeks, which is why this guide explains how to find the rate for your product rather than quoting one. Sources used here were checked on 6 November 2026; confirm the rate in force with your customs broker before pricing against it.

## Two components: the base rate and the additional tariffs

The duty you pay on a Chinese-origin good has two parts:

1. **The base HTS rate.** The classification for your product in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, looked up by its 10-digit HTS code at hts.usitc.gov (the U.S. International Trade Commission's official lookup).
2. **Additional tariffs on Chinese-origin goods.** Layers added by government action — Section 301 duties among them, listed by HTS subheading in the USTR notices and the HTS tables. Their coverage and rates are set and changed by government action.

A quotation priced in 2025 or earlier is not evidence of the duty you will pay in 2026. Rates and coverage move.

## How to find the rate for your product

1. **Get the HTS code.** Determine the 10-digit classification for your product — your broker confirms it, or work it up through our HS code research as working classification, subject to confirmation by the importer or customs broker.
2. **Look up the base rate** for that subheading at hts.usitc.gov.
3. **Check additional tariffs.** The ITC publishes a table of HTS subheadings covered by additional tariffs on products of China; your broker confirms which layers apply to your code and the rates in force.
4. **Add the entry fees.** The Merchandise Processing Fee on most formal entries and, for cargo arriving by vessel, the Harbor Maintenance Fee. Confirm current rates with your broker.
5. **Record the date checked.** Tariff citations age; the assumption belongs in the quotation with its date.

## Origin matters more than people expect

Additional tariffs apply by origin. Goods made in China but shipped from elsewhere do not necessarily escape them, and country of origin is determined by where the goods are manufactured, not where they are shipped from. Transshipment to avoid tariffs is not a strategy; it is a violation with penalties.

## What a supplier can and cannot tell you

A factory can tell you the export price and the material composition. It cannot give you a binding duty determination for the United States — that work belongs to the importer and a licensed customs broker. A working classification and a landed-cost estimate with dated tariff assumptions are planning tools, not rulings.

## Sources

- Table of China tariff coverage by HTS subheading: published by the USITC alongside the HTS release
- Section 301 actions and notices: [ustr.gov](https://ustr.gov/) (Office of the U.S. Trade Representative)
- Entry fees and guidance: [cbp.gov](https://www.cbp.gov/) (U.S. Customs and Border Protection)
