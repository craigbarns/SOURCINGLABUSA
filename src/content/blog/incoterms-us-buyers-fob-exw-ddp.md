The Incoterm in a quotation decides where the supplier's costs stop and yours begin. An FOB price and an EXW price do not cover the same costs, and a DDP price hides most of them. Comparing two quotes on different terms without converting them is how margins get misjudged. Official guidance on the Incoterms rules is published by the International Chamber of Commerce.

## EXW — Ex Works: the factory door

You take the goods at the supplier's premises. The price includes the product and nothing else: export packing, the export licence, trucking to the port, the ocean leg and duties are all yours or your agents'.

EXW is workable when you have a freight forwarder in China who handles export regularly. It is a poor term when you do not: arranging export clearance yourself from abroad is slow and error-prone.

## FOB — Free On Board: the vessel

The supplier delivers the goods onto the vessel at the named port, cleared for export. From that point, freight, insurance, destination charges, duties and entry are yours.

FOB is the practical default for most U.S. importers buying from China: the supplier handles the Chinese-side costs it can handle best, and the freight side stays under your control with your own forwarder.

## DDP — Delivered Duty Paid: your door, duties included

The supplier arranges delivery to your address with duties paid. It reads as the safest term, and it is the least transparent one: every cost line — freight, duties, broker, delivery — sits inside one number, priced by the supplier with a markup on each.

DDP is reasonable for small shipments and samples. For repeat production orders it removes your ability to compare freight, verify duties, or see whether the landed cost is efficient. Note also that a supplier who is not a U.S. importer of record cannot "handle your customs entry" — someone must be the importer of record, and that party's obligations are what DDP really bundles.

## Comparing quotes on different terms

Convert every quotation to the same basis before comparing unit prices:

1. Take the quoted unit price and the commercial term.
2. Add the cost lines the term leaves out: inland China, export clearance, ocean freight per carton, insurance, duties at the confirmed rate, broker and local delivery.
3. Divide by the units that fit the carton — not by the units ordered — to see the real unit comparison.

A landed-cost model does exactly this arithmetic, line by line, with the assumption and the party that carries it. Ask for it to be written into the quotation if you are comparing terms.

## One line that prevents disputes

Whichever term you choose, the quotation should state it with the named place — FOB Shanghai is not FOB Ningbo — and the parties for transport and entry. Incoterms allocate costs between named parties; they do not transfer export obligations magically, and they never make the supplier your customs broker.
