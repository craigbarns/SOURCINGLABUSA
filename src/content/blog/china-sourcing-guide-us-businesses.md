To source products from China, define the product and intended U.S. use, identify suitable suppliers, compare written quotations, approve samples, and agree production checkpoints and delivery responsibilities before ordering. Keep a record of the evidence supporting each decision. A supplier listing, a low price or an attractive sample alone does not settle whether an order is ready.

This guide is a buying workflow for U.S. brands and business customers. It connects the decisions that often sit in separate emails: the product brief, supplier review, sample approval, price basis and shipment responsibilities. Start with the [editable product brief](/resources/product-sourcing-brief).

## How do you find manufacturers that fit the product?

Search by production requirements, not just the product name. For a printed box, structure, material, print process and assembly may distinguish suitable suppliers. For clothing, fabric, construction, size grading and decoration matter. Create a shortlist from relevant directories, trade events, referrals and direct manufacturer research, then test each candidate against the same brief.

Record the source of each lead, the business name, the contact and the reason it appears relevant. Ask which production steps the company performs and which it subcontracts. A factory and a trading company can both be relevant; the decision depends on the proposed arrangement and evidence. Our [supplier qualification guide](/blog/verify-chinese-supplier) shows what to request before comparing price.

## What should happen at each sourcing stage?

The table below is an original Sourcing Lab USA planning workflow, introduced with this guide. It is a decision aid, not a certification or a claim about completed projects. Assign a person to each open decision rather than assuming the supplier will resolve it.

| Stage | Decision to make | Record to keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specification | What must the product do, and what may change? | Numbered brief and revision |
| Discovery | Which candidates appear to fit the process and quantity? | Shortlist with source and production scope |
| Qualification | Which claims are supported, missing or contradictory? | Evidence register and open questions |
| RFQ | Are proposals based on the same requirements? | Quote comparison and exclusions |
| Samples | Which features are approved for production? | Sample reference, photographs and approval notes |
| Negotiation | Which changes affect price, MOQ or risk? | Written alternatives and revised quotation |
| Production | What will be checked, by whom and when? | Order-specific checkpoint plan |
| Release | Have discrepancies and required documents been resolved? | Release decision and unresolved items |
| Consolidation, if relevant | Can components be combined under agreed arrangements? | Packing list, handoff owner and confirmed costs |
| Transport and receipt | Who arranges each step and verifies receipt? | Shipping instructions and receiving record |

These are decision stages, not a promised schedule or a list of services automatically included in a quote. External inspection, testing, consolidation and transport must have an agreed provider and scope when required.

## What makes two quotations comparable?

Use one revision of the [RFQ checklist](/blog/china-sourcing-rfq-checklist). Separate product price, setup, samples, testing or inspection, and delivery costs. Record quantity by variant, payment terms, quote validity, proposed substitutions and the exact delivery basis. A cheaper quote for a thinner board or a different fabric is an alternative specification, not evidence of a saving.

For example, a packaging supplier might quote a box and insert together while another quotes only the box. Add a component-completeness column before comparing their totals. The same principle applies to garment labels, trims and individual packing. Use the [landed-cost worked example](/blog/calculating-landed-costs-merchandise) to keep missing amounts visible.

## How do U.S. import requirements enter the process?

Bring the exact product description, materials, intended use, origin and destination to the relevant import and product specialists before approving production. CBP explains that [importer responsibility remains with the importer even when a customs broker is used](https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1169?language=en_US). A supply quotation should therefore name responsibilities, not leave the importer unspecified.

Use the [official U.S. tariff schedule](https://hts.usitc.gov/) as a reference for classification review. This guide does not assign a tariff code or rate. Product requirements and charges depend on the specific goods and current rules; ask a licensed customs broker or qualified product specialist where appropriate.

## When is using a sourcing partner worthwhile?

A partner can be useful when your team needs help turning specifications, supplier discussions, samples and production decisions into an agreed order. Direct sourcing may fit a team with the time and expertise to perform those tasks itself. Compare the actual work, contracting parties and costs in both routes. ## What should you do when information is missing?

Keep the affected decision open. An unverified company identity calls for further qualification; a sample that differs from the drawing calls for a corrected sample or an explicit change approval. A missing transport cost calls for an estimate with an owner and expiry date. Use the [quality checkpoint worksheet](/blog/quality-control-checklist) to connect these open items to the decision they block.
